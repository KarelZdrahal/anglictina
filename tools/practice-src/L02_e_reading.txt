EARLY BIRDS AND NIGHT OWLS: IS YOUR BODY CLOCK TO BLAME?
Some people jump out of bed at six, full of energy, while others hit the snooze button again and again and don't feel properly awake until lunchtime. For a long time, people who got up late were described as lazy. Today, scientists say the truth is more complicated: much of it depends on our "chronotype" – our natural body clock.
Researchers believe that about a quarter of people are clear early birds and another quarter are true night owls. Everyone else is somewhere in between. Our chronotype is partly in our genes, but it also changes with age. Small children tend to wake up very early, teenagers are inclined to stay up late and sleep until noon, and many older people go back to being early birds.
The problem is that most schools and workplaces are organised for early birds. A night owl who has to start work at seven will often feel tired, make more mistakes and need more coffee. Some studies even suggest that night owls who keep fighting against their body clock are more likely to suffer from stress.
That is one reason why more companies now offer flexible working hours. "We don't care when people work, we care about results," says the director of a software company in Brno. "Our night owls tend to do their best work in the evening, and that's fine."
So can a night owl become an early bird? Experts say small changes can help: get plenty of daylight in the morning, avoid screens late at night and try to stick to the same routine, even at weekends. But don't expect a miracle – your body clock may move by an hour or so, not more.

THE FOUR-DAY WEEK: TOO GOOD TO BE TRUE?
Imagine working four days a week for the same salary. For thousands of employees around the world, this is no longer a dream. In recent years, companies in Britain, Iceland, Spain and other countries have taken part in trials of a four-day working week, and the results have surprised many people.
In one of the biggest trials, in the UK, about sixty companies reduced working hours by twenty percent without cutting pay. After six months, most of them decided to continue. Employees said they felt less stressed, slept better and had more time for their families and hobbies. Interestingly, the companies did not lose money – in many cases productivity stayed the same or even improved.
How is that possible? Managers say that people tend to waste less time when they have fewer hours. Meetings become shorter, colleagues stop checking social media at their desks, and people focus on the most important tasks. "On Fridays I used to spend half the day answering unnecessary emails," says Sarah, a marketing manager. "Now I simply don't have time for that."
However, the four-day week does not work for everyone. Hospitals, shops and restaurants need staff every day, so they have to organise shifts very carefully. Some employees also found that they had to work longer and more intense days to finish everything in four days. "On Thursdays I'm exhausted," admits one engineer. "But a three-day weekend is worth it."
Critics also point out that the trials were voluntary, so the companies that took part were probably the ones most likely to succeed. Still, the idea is spreading, and for many workers, a better work-life balance is now more important than a higher salary.

NIGHTS ON THE WARD
Eva Horáková has been a nurse at a hospital in Ostrava for nineteen years, and for most of that time she has worked nights. Her shift starts at seven in the evening and finishes at seven in the morning.
"People always ask me how I can do it," she says. "The truth is, I'm a natural night owl. Even as a child I would read under the covers with a torch until two in the morning. My mum was always telling me to go to sleep!"
A typical night is never really typical. Eva will usually start by talking to the day nurses about each patient. Then she checks medicines and makes sure everyone is comfortable. Around midnight the ward tends to get quieter, and that's when she catches up on paperwork. "But you can never relax completely. Something always happens at three in the morning – that's the rule."
Night work is not easy on the body. Eva admits that she used to struggle with sleep during the day. "In my first year I kept waking up at noon because of the noise outside. Now I have thick curtains and earplugs, and I stick to a strict routine: home, a light breakfast, shower, and bed by nine." She also makes a habit of going for a long walk before every shift, whatever the weather.
What does she like about nights? "There's a special atmosphere. The team is small, so we really help each other. And patients are often more inclined to talk at night – they're worried, they can't sleep, and they want someone to listen."
Would she ever go back to days? Eva laughs. "My husband keeps asking me that. Maybe when I'm older. For now, the night is my time."

HOW TO BREAK A BAD HABIT – AND MAKE A GOOD ONE
We all have habits we would like to break: biting our nails, eating sweets in front of the TV, or checking our phones every five minutes. And we all have habits we would like to start, like exercising regularly or going to bed earlier. So why is it so hard to change?
According to journalist Charles Duhigg, every habit works like a loop with three parts. First there is a cue – a trigger, such as feeling bored or arriving home from work. Then comes the routine – the behaviour itself, for example opening the fridge. Finally there is the reward – the pleasant feeling we get afterwards. After many repetitions, the brain connects the cue directly with the reward, and the behaviour becomes automatic.
The good news is that we can use the same loop to our advantage. Experts say you shouldn't try to simply stop a bad habit; it is more effective to replace the routine with a better one. If you tend to eat biscuits when you are stressed at work, keep a bag of nuts on your desk or go for a five-minute walk instead. The cue and the reward stay the same – only the behaviour changes.
To build a new habit, start small. Instead of promising yourself to run ten kilometres every day, put on your trainers and walk around the block. Connect the new habit to something you already do: "After I make my morning coffee, I'll do two minutes of stretching." And be patient. A famous study from University College London found that it takes an average of sixty-six days for a new behaviour to become automatic – not twenty-one, as many people believe.
Finally, don't give up if you have a bad day. Missing one day doesn't ruin a habit. Missing a week might.

ONE YEAR OF WORKING FROM HOME – AN HONEST REVIEW
Exactly a year ago, my company told us we could work remotely full-time. I was delighted. No more crowded trams, no more forty-minute commute, no more colleagues talking loudly on the phone next to me. Twelve months later, here is my honest review.
Let's start with the good things. I have gained almost seven hours a week that I used to spend travelling. I now make time for breakfast with my kids, and I go for a run at lunchtime. I'm also more productive: I can concentrate for hours without interruptions, and I tend to finish my most difficult tasks before ten in the morning.
But there have been problems too. In the first months, I fell into some really bad habits. I would roll out of bed at five to nine and start work in my pyjamas. I kept eating snacks all day because the kitchen was only five steps away. And because my laptop was always on the kitchen table, I never really finished work. I'd answer emails at ten at night and on Sunday afternoons. My work-life balance was worse than ever.
The other thing I didn't expect was loneliness. I miss the small conversations by the coffee machine. On some days, the only person I spoke to was the delivery driver.
So what has changed? I now have a proper desk in a small spare room, and when I close the door at six, the working day is over. I get dressed every morning as if I were going to the office. And I go to the office once a week to see my team – I actually look forward to it now.
Would I go back to the office full-time? Definitely not. But working from home is not as easy as it looks.

MY GRANDFATHER'S WORKING LIFE
My grandfather Josef worked in the same steelworks for forty-two years. When I was a child, he would sit me on his knee and tell me stories about his job, and I never got tired of listening.
He used to get up at half past four every morning. My grandmother would make him coffee and two thick slices of bread with lard, and he would cycle six kilometres to the factory, whatever the weather. He was a real creature of habit: the same breakfast, the same bicycle, the same route. Even after he retired, he kept on waking up at half past four. "My body doesn't know I'm a pensioner," he used to joke.
The work was hard and dangerous. There were no flexible working hours and nobody had heard of work-life balance. Workers clocked in at six and clocked out at two, and if you were late three times, you lost part of your salary. Grandad was a stickler for punctuality – in forty-two years he was never late once. He was proud of that.
But he also remembered good things. Colleagues helped each other, and older workers showed the young ones the ropes. On Fridays, the whole team would go to the pub near the gate for a beer before going home. Many of his colleagues were his best friends for life.
When I tell him about my job – working remotely, video calls, emails at midnight – he shakes his head. "You young people are always looking at your screens," he says. "We worked hard, but when we went home, work stayed at the factory." I'm inclined to think he has a point.
