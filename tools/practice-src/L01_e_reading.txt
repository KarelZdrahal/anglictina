WHAT YOUR BIRTH ORDER SAYS ABOUT YOU
Are you the oldest child in your family, the youngest, or somewhere in the middle? Some psychologists believe that our position in the family influences our personality. First-born children are often described as responsible, organised and ambitious. Because parents usually have more time for their first child, these children often get a lot of attention and they learn to follow rules. Many of them become leaders – a surprising number of company directors and politicians are oldest children.
Middle children, on the other hand, are often described as easy-going and sociable. They have to share their parents' attention with older and younger brothers and sisters, so they learn to negotiate and to get on well with different kinds of people. They are often good listeners and loyal friends.
And the youngest? They are said to be cheerful, outgoing and sometimes a little rebellious. Parents are usually more relaxed with their last child, so the youngest often have more freedom. They like taking risks and they are often creative.
However, not all experts agree. A large study from 2015, which looked at more than 20,000 people, found only very small differences between first-born and younger children. The researchers say that our personality depends much more on our genes, our friends and our experiences than on our position in the family. So if you are the oldest child and you are not organised at all, don't worry – you're completely normal!

INTROVERTS AT WORK: THE QUIET POWER
In many offices, the people who talk the most in meetings get the most attention. They are confident, outgoing and good at selling their ideas. But are they always the best workers? According to writer Susan Cain, the answer is no. In her popular book "Quiet", she explains that about one third of people are introverts – people who feel more comfortable in small groups and who need time alone to recharge their energy.
Introverts are often seen as shy, but shyness and introversion are not the same thing. A shy person is afraid of what other people think, while an introvert simply prefers a calm environment. Many introverts are excellent speakers; they just need more time to prepare. They are usually good listeners, they think carefully before they act, and they often notice details that others miss.
Today, more and more companies are trying to create offices that work for everyone. Some firms are building quiet rooms where employees can concentrate without noise. Others are changing the way they run meetings: instead of asking people to speak immediately, managers send questions in advance, so that quieter colleagues can prepare their answers. "We are losing great ideas if only the loudest people speak," says one HR manager from Prague.
So next time a colleague is quiet in a meeting, don't think that they have nothing to say. They are probably listening – and thinking.

MY NEW FLATMATE
I'm writing this blog post from the kitchen of my new flat in Brno. I moved here two months ago for my job, and because rents are high, I'm sharing the flat with a guy called Ondřej. Before I met him, I was a bit worried. I'm quite an organised person – I like my things in their place – and I was afraid that my flatmate would be messy and lazy.
Well, Ondřej is not exactly tidy. At the moment there are three of his coffee cups on the table next to me, and his bike is standing in the hall. He's also a little absent-minded: he regularly forgets his keys, and twice he has left the oven on. But he's also one of the most generous and thoughtful people I know. When I had flu last month, he cooked soup for me every day and went to the chemist's for my medicine. He's very easy-going, too – we've never had a single argument.
These days I'm learning to be more relaxed. I don't mind the coffee cups so much any more, and Ondřej is trying to be more careful – this week he's washing the dishes every evening without me asking. I think we're a good team. My mum always says that you can't change people, but you can learn to live with them. I'm starting to think she's right.

THE CHANGING FACE OF FRIENDSHIP
How many friends do you have? If you count your contacts on social media, the answer might be several hundred. But according to the British scientist Robin Dunbar, most people can only have about five very close friends and around 150 people they really know. Dunbar's research shows that this number has not changed much in thousands of years.
What is changing, however, is the way we keep in touch. Twenty years ago, people usually met their friends in person or talked on the phone. Today, many of us are communicating mainly through messages and short videos. Some experts are worried that these online conversations are less deep and that people are becoming lonelier. A recent survey found that young adults now spend about 40 percent less time with friends face to face than young people did in the early 2000s.
Others are more optimistic. They point out that technology helps people to stay close to friends who live far away – for example, people who are working or studying abroad. Shy people also often find it easier to start a conversation online.
Dunbar himself has a simple piece of advice: friendships need time. If we don't see a friend for a long time, the friendship slowly becomes weaker. "You can keep a friendship alive with messages for a while," he says, "but sooner or later you need to sit down together, have a meal and laugh."

A DAY IN THE LIFE: VETERINARY NURSE
Kristýna Malá is 29 and works as a veterinary nurse at an animal clinic in Olomouc. "People often think my job is just playing with puppies," she laughs. "It isn't!"
Kristýna's day usually starts at 7.30. She checks the animals that are staying at the clinic overnight, gives them their medicine and cleans their cages. At eight the clinic opens and the phone starts ringing. "Most mornings are crazy. Owners are calling, dogs are barking, and somebody is always running late," she says. She helps the vets during operations, takes blood samples and explains to owners how to look after their pets at home.
To do this job, you need to be patient and calm. "Animals feel when you are nervous," Kristýna explains. "And owners are often very worried, so you need to be sensitive and a good listener, too." She also says that you can't be squeamish – you see a lot of blood.
This month is especially busy, because one of her colleagues is on maternity leave and the clinic is looking for a new nurse. Kristýna is working longer hours than usual, but she doesn't complain. "I love what I do. When a sick animal goes home healthy and the owner is smiling, I know why I get up so early."
In her free time she goes running and, of course, spends time with her own two cats.

CAN YOU CHANGE YOUR PERSONALITY?
For a long time, psychologists believed that our personality was fixed by the age of about thirty. If you were shy and disorganised at thirty, you would probably stay shy and disorganised for the rest of your life. Recent research, however, suggests that this is not true.
Studies that follow the same people for many years show that most of us are slowly changing all the time. As people get older, they usually become more responsible, calmer and kinder. Psychologists call this "the maturity principle". Important life events – starting a new job, becoming a parent, moving to another country – can also change the way we think and behave.
Even more interesting is the question of whether we can change on purpose. In one American experiment, students who wanted to become more outgoing or more organised received small weekly tasks, for example "say hello to a stranger" or "plan tomorrow before you go to bed". After fifteen weeks, many of them really were different – and their friends noticed the change, too.
The researchers say that the secret is not to try to become a completely new person overnight. Small, regular actions are much more effective. "Personality is like a muscle," says one of the scientists. "If you train it a little every day, it gets stronger."
So if you think you are too impatient, too lazy or too stubborn, there is good news: you are not stuck. You are already changing – and you can decide in which direction.
